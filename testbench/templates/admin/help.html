Draft, live and closed

What each study status means for participants, data integrity, and researcher workflows.

Status What participants see What you can change When to use
Draft

“This study is being prepared”

Participants who open the link cannot begin or submit responses. No session records are created.

Full editing allowed

Add, edit, or remove questions, prototypes, modules, and branding without restriction. Use Preview to pilot test yourself.

Study design, writing tasks, configuring answer keys, uploading prototype HTML files, and team dry runs.
Live

Active study entrance

Participants enter via the study URL (and shared passcode if required), answer questions, and submit timing and click data.

Caution on editing

Changing questions or variant files while live can invalidate comparisons. The editor shows a confirmation warning if you modify live studies.

Fielding the study to participants, distributing the participant link or QR code, and collecting data.
Closed

“Study completed”

Nobody new can enter and active participants cannot resume. The study entrance is sealed.

Read-only data & export

All reports, session charts, and CSV exports remain open. You can reopen the study anytime by setting status back to Live.

When participant recruitment targets are met or your research study concludes.
Offline

“This study is not available”

Neutral holding message shown if a platform operator places the study on administrative hold.

Preserved in place

Responses and configurations are kept intact. The operator or superadmin can restore the study once reviewed.

Investigating reports, auditing safety, or repairing broken external resources.

You can switch status at any time using the status badge dropdown in the top header or on the Launch page.

Choosing a module

Pick the right research method for your questions.

Module Question answered Input needed Deliverables & output

A/B test

ab

Which of two designs is easier to use?

Compares task performance across two interactive HTML prototypes (Variant A and Variant B).

Two prototype HTML files (e.g. in prototypes/) and 1 to 5 task prompts with optional automated URL/click answer keys.

Speed, success rate, perceived ease ratings, sign test p-values, order effect checks, and a plain automated verdict.

Survey

survey

Who are your participants, and what is hard for them?

Screening, satisfaction metrics, demographic capture, or contextual post-task questions.

Questions formatted as single select, multi select, open text, rating scales, or matrix grids.

Frequency distributions, response percentages, free-text answer logs, and conditional routing tags.

First click

first_click

Where do people click first to accomplish a task?

Evaluates initial mental models, visual hierarchy, and affordance on static interfaces.

An uploaded page image/mockup and one or more target bounding boxes (expected click regions).

Interactive click coordinate heatmaps, percentage of clicks inside the expected area, and median time to first click.

Card sort

card_sort

How do people categorize and conceptualize your content?

Informs information architecture, site navigation, and product taxonomy.

15 to 40 content cards. Choose closed (fixed categories), open (participant-named), or hybrid.

Co-occurrence matrix, category agreement ratios (≥70% clear home, <50% uncertain), and popular card groupings.

Tree test

tree_test

Where would people navigate in a text-only hierarchy?

Tests menu and taxonomy findability isolated from visual design, styling, and banners.

A nested menu tree (sections, categories, pages) and tasks pointing to one or more correct destination nodes.

Overall task success rate, directness percentage (navigating without backtracking), and weakest task identification.

Modules can be reordered by dragging them on the Build page.

Reading results

Every verdict and summary sentence is generated by fixed deterministic rules, never invented by a language model.

Term / Metric Statistical & Algorithmic Rule What it means in reports
Indicative

Sample size threshold

Flags reports until at least 8 participants (or custom min_participants in A/B rules) have completed the module.

Data collected so far is preliminary. No design or variant is declared a definitive winner until the threshold is crossed.
Intervals

Standard error bounds

Calculates the 95% confidence interval range around observed metric differences.

If the confidence interval spans across zero (e.g. -4s to +6s), the difference could be due to random variation, and the report notes this.
A/B verdict

Tri-metric synthesis

Evaluates three independent performance dimensions: task completion speed, success rate, and perceived ease score.

Summarizes into clean verdicts (e.g. Variant A is faster and easier, or No clear difference). Never claims more than the least certain check supports.
Order effect

Counterbalance comparison

Tests whether participants who saw Variant A first performed differently from those who saw Variant B first.

Reported as an empirical observation (e.g. Variant A was faster in both presentation orders) without speculative bias claims.
Drop-off

Abandonment heuristic

Triggers when ≥2 participants and ≥20% of entrants abandon at the same step.

Highlights potential roadblocks or cognitive fatigue where participants gave up before finishing.
Card sort agreement

Consensus threshold

Measures percentage of participants assigning a card to the dominant category.

≥70% denotes a clear home; 50%–69% indicates moderate consensus; <50% denotes no clear home, suggesting ambiguity or missing categories.
First click

Target hit rate & latency

Records coordinate of initial click on the mockup and calculates median millisecond duration.

First-click success strongly correlates with final task completion in live systems.
Tree test directness

Path efficiency ratio

Tracks whether the user visited only parent/ancestor nodes of the target without navigating backward.

High directness indicates intuitive nomenclature; low directness highlights confusing branch labels.
Sign test

Binomial two-sided test

Evaluates paired participant directions (which variant was faster or easier for each individual).

Provides a non-parametric p-value assessing whether one variant reliably outperforms the other across individuals.

Reports can be viewed online or downloaded as raw CSV datasets from the Results page.

Editing as YAML

For researchers and engineers who prefer version control and text files.

Every study is backed by plain text YAML files stored directly on disk:

  • project.yaml: Defines study title, status, brand colors, participant identification mode, and consent statements.
  • modules/<module_id>.yaml: Configures individual module parameters, question sets, prototypes, and decision rules.
  • prototypes/: Contains standalone HTML, CSS, and JS assets used by A/B testing and interactive modules.

Every module editor in the Studio has an Advanced tab with the exact YAML file. You can edit via the form UI or directly in YAML. Every save validates the entire study schema; if a syntax error or broken reference exists, no changes are committed to disk and line-numbered errors are reported immediately.

Access, identity and privacy

How authentication, passcodes, and participant privacy are safeguarded.

Identity Mode How participants sign in Privacy & Data Security
Anonymous

Automatic resume code

Participants receive a randomized alphanumeric code (e.g. WXYZ-4567) to resume their session if disconnected.

Zero PII collected. No names, email addresses, or IP addresses are permanently associated with responses.
Passcodes

Shared study gatekeeper

Configured on the Launch page. Participants enter the shared secret to access the study.

Stored using salted cryptographic hashes (PBKDF2/SHA256). Passcodes can be updated or revoked anytime without exposing the raw secret.
Participant code / Email

Pre-assigned identifiers

Useful when linking to external panel providers, user pools, or internal corporate email domains.

Validated against regex patterns or domain whitelists. Responses remain strictly isolated within each study's private database.

All participant sessions and module responses are stored locally in isolated SQLite databases per study.
